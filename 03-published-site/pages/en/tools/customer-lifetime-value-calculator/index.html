What is customer lifetime value?

Customer lifetime value, or CLV, is the total margin a customer leaves behind between their first order and the day they stop coming back. It rests on three things: how much they spend per order, how often they order in a year, and how many years they stay. As long as you only look at the first sale, you underestimate the customer and cap your acquisition budget far below what you could afford.

What is customer lifetime value (CLV)?

Customer lifetime value is the total profit you can expect from one customer over the whole time they keep buying from you. Not one order. Every order that customer will ever place, minus what it costs you to serve them.

That single number changes how you run a store, for three reasons.

  • It sets the maximum you can pay to acquire a customer, which is the only thing that makes an advertising budget rational instead of superstitious
  • It measures profitability across the relationship instead of per transaction, so a first order sold near break-even stops looking like a mistake
  • It shows which customers deserve retention spend, and the gap between your best and worst cohorts is usually wider than the gap between your best and worst products

Most stores never calculate it, then wonder how a competitor outbids them on every keyword and still makes money. That competitor is not luckier. They know the second order exists and they price acquisition against it.

CLV vs LTV: is there a difference?

No. CLV means customer lifetime value, LTV means lifetime value, and the two are used interchangeably in e-commerce and in SaaS. Anyone who tells you one is strictly per customer and the other per account is describing a convention, not a rule.

The distinction that does matter is revenue CLV versus margin CLV. The same customer can be worth 600 EUR of revenue and 270 EUR of margin. Since your acquisition cost is paid in real money, only the margin version can be compared with it. This calculator works on margin, which is why it asks for your gross margin instead of just your revenue.

What CLV is not

Three numbers get mistaken for lifetime value, and each one leads to a different bad decision.

  • Average order value measures one transaction. A store can have an excellent average order value and a terrible lifetime value if nobody comes back
  • Repeat purchase rate tells you how many customers order again, not how much profit that represents. Twenty percent of customers reordering a 12 EUR refill is not the same business as ten percent reordering a 200 EUR kit
  • Total revenue per customer ignores what the orders cost you. It is the version that makes an unprofitable store look like a growth story

Lifetime value is the only one of the four that can be compared with an acquisition cost, which is the whole reason to calculate it.

Why CLV matters for dropshippers and e-commerce stores

Dropshipping runs on thin margins and rising acquisition costs. A store that only breaks even on the first order has no room left: one bad week of ad performance and the whole thing is underwater. The stores that survive are the ones where the second and third order exist, because that is where the margin lives.

Take two stores with the same 40 EUR acquisition cost and the same 35 EUR margin per order. The first sells a decorative item bought once: lifetime value 35 EUR, a 0.9:1 ratio, dead on arrival. The second sells a consumable reordered three times a year for two years: lifetime value around 200 EUR, a 5:1 ratio, and enough room to outbid the first store on every single ad auction.

Same margin per order. Same acquisition cost. Opposite outcomes, decided by the product catalogue before a single ad was written.

How to calculate customer lifetime value: the formula

Simple CLV formula

The standard customer lifetime value calculation multiplies four variables:

CLV = average order value × purchase frequency per year × customer lifespan in years × gross margin

With a 100 EUR average order value, 4 orders a year, a 3 year lifespan and a 20% gross margin, CLV is 240 EUR. That is the profit one customer leaves behind across the relationship, and the ceiling for everything you spend to win them.

Average order value is total revenue divided by the number of orders. It is the easiest variable to move in the short term and the one most stores neglect.

Purchase frequency is how many times the average customer buys in a year. It is decided mostly by what you sell: a coffee brand is reordered monthly, a suitcase brand every five years.

Customer lifespan is how long a customer keeps buying before going quiet. It comes from your churn: lifespan = 1 / annual churn rate. If 30% of customers never come back within a year, lifespan is 1 / 0.30, so 3.3 years.

Gross margin is what stays after the cost of goods, shipping and payment fees. If you are not sure of yours, run it through the dropshipping profit margin calculator first, then come back here with a clean number.

This calculator asks for your retention rate rather than a lifespan in years, because retention is something you can pull out of your own order history, while a lifespan in years is usually a guess. It converts it for you with 1 / (1 - retention), which is the same arithmetic seen from the other side.

Predictive CLV formula (advanced)

The formula above treats every customer as the average customer. Predictive CLV does not. It uses probabilistic models, typically BG/NBD for how often someone buys and a Gamma-Gamma model for how much they spend, to estimate a value per customer from their individual transaction history. The reference work is Fader and Hardie's research on probability models for customer base analysis, and it is what large retailers run in production.

It is more accurate, and it costs more. You need clean order-level data, a pipeline that refreshes it, and someone who can read the output. For a store doing a few hundred orders a month, the simple formula gets you within range of the truth and the decision it drives, how much you can pay for a customer, is identical. Move to predictive CLV when segment-level precision starts changing your budget allocation, not before.

Four mistakes that break a CLV calculation

The formula is simple enough that most errors come from the inputs, not the maths.

  • Using revenue instead of margin. The most common one, and it inflates the answer by whatever your cost of goods is. It turns a 2:1 business into a 5:1 fantasy
  • Guessing the lifespan. Three years sounds reasonable and is usually optimistic. Derive it from churn instead: 1 divided by the share of customers who do not come back
  • Using an all-time average. A store that changed its product mix or doubled its prices last year is averaging two different businesses. Use the last twelve months
  • Forgetting the cost of keeping the customer. Support, returns, loyalty discounts and platform fees are paid every year the relationship lasts, and on low margin catalogues they eat a surprising share of the lifetime value

Get those four right and a back of the envelope calculation is accurate enough to set an acquisition budget. Get them wrong and no amount of modelling sophistication will save the answer.

How to use this CLV calculator (step by step)

Step 1: enter your average order value (AOV)

The average amount a customer spends in a single purchase. Divide total revenue by total orders over the same period, or read it in Shopify under Analytics, then Reports, in the sales reports. Fifty thousand euros of revenue across 500 orders gives a 100 EUR average order value. Use a full quarter so a single promotion does not distort it.

Step 2: enter your purchase frequency

How many orders the average customer places in a year. Divide orders by unique customers over twelve months: 200 orders from 100 customers is a frequency of 2. Shopify shows the raw material for it under Analytics, Reports, customer behaviour, next to your repeat purchase rate. If you launched recently, use what you have and be conservative rather than hopeful.

Step 3: enter your gross margin

The percentage left after the cost of goods sold, shipping and payment fees. A product that costs you 20 EUR and sells for 50 EUR carries a 60% margin. This is the field that turns a flattering revenue number into a figure you can actually compare with your acquisition cost.

Step 4: enter your annual retention rate

The share of customers who are still buying a year later. Take the customers who bought more than twelve months ago and count how many ordered again since: that ratio is your retention. On a dropshipping or single-product store it is usually in the single digits, and 5% to 10% coming back is already decent. Established brands with a real catalogue, consumables or subscriptions reach 20%, 40% and beyond. The calculator turns it into an average lifespan with 1 / (1 - retention), so 40% retention means 1.7 years.

Optional: acquisition cost, service costs and discount rate

Adding your customer acquisition cost unlocks the CLV:CAC ratio, the payback period and the maximum CAC you can afford. Service costs cover support, returns and loyalty programmes, paid every year the customer stays. The discount rate brings future margin back to today's value; leave it at zero unless you are building a financial model.

Reading your results: CLV, annual value and CLV:CAC ratio

The headline figure is your customer lifetime value in margin. Under it sits the annual customer value, the margin one customer produces in a single year, which is the number your cash flow actually sees.

Then come the ratios. Net CLV is what remains once acquisition is paid for. The CLV:CAC ratio compares value to cost and drives the badge: critical below 1:1, low between 1:1 and 3:1, healthy between 3:1 and 5:1, and above 5:1 you are under-investing in acquisition. Payback tells you how many months it takes to earn your acquisition cost back, and the retention strip at the bottom shows what your customer would be worth at 20%, 40% or 70% retention, so you can see the value of a retention point before you spend anything to gain it.

A worked example, start to finish

Take a skincare store. Average order value 68 EUR, customers order 2.4 times a year, gross margin 45%, annual retention 40%, and support plus returns cost about 6 EUR per customer per year. Acquisition currently costs 32 EUR.

Annual margin per customer is 68 x 2.4 x 0.45, minus 6, so 67 EUR. Retention of 40% gives a lifespan of 1.7 years, which puts lifetime value at roughly 112 EUR and net value at 80 EUR once acquisition is paid. The ratio lands at 3.5:1, healthy, with payback in about six months.

Now change one variable. Retention at 60% instead of 40% takes the lifespan to 2.5 years and lifetime value to about 168 EUR, a 3.4:1 ratio at a much higher acquisition cost: this store could pay 50 EUR per customer instead of 32 and still be in the safe zone. That is 18 EUR more per customer to outbid competitors with, bought entirely with retention work rather than ad budget.

What is a good CLV for an e-commerce store?

CLV benchmarks by industry

There is no universal good number, because CLV is the product of a category, a price point and a reorder cycle. These ranges are what stores in each vertical typically report, on margin, over the full customer relationship.

IndustryTypical CLVWhy
Fashion and apparel150 to 300 EURSeasonal buying, moderate margin, strong brand pull
Beauty and skincare200 to 400 EURConsumable products, natural reorder cycle
Electronics and accessories300 to 600 EURInfrequent purchases, higher margin per order
Supplements and nutrition400 to 800 EURMonthly consumption, subscription friendly
General dropshipping50 to 150 EURLow frequency, thin margin, weak brand attachment

Use them as a sanity check, not as a target. A 90 EUR CLV is excellent if your acquisition cost is 20 EUR and catastrophic if it is 100 EUR. The absolute number means nothing until you put it next to what you pay to get the customer.

CLV:CAC ratio: the key metric to watch

Three to one is the threshold everyone quotes, and for once the consensus is right: roughly a third of the value goes to fixed costs, logistics and things going wrong. What matters is knowing what to do at each level.

CLV:CAC ratioWhat it meansWhat to do
Above 5:1You are leaving growth on the tableRaise bids, widen audiences, test new channels
3:1 to 5:1Healthy and scalablePush volume, protect retention
1.5:1 to 3:1Viable but tightWork on average order value and repeat rate first
Below 1.5:1UnsustainablePause scaling, fix the offer or the product mix
Below 1:1CriticalEvery new sale deepens the loss, stop the spend

If your ratio is below 1.5:1, you are losing money on every customer you buy. Pause the scaling, fix the offer, the margin or the product mix, and only then turn the spend back on. Payback: the constraint a good CLV can hide

A lifetime value spread over four years does not pay this month's invoices. Payback divides your acquisition cost by the monthly margin a customer produces, and it answers a different question: not whether the customer is worth it, but whether you can afford to wait.

A store that recovers its acquisition cost in three months can reinvest the same euro four times a year. A store that takes eighteen months funds its growth out of working capital, and grows exactly as fast as its bank balance allows. Two stores with an identical 4:1 ratio can therefore scale at completely different speeds.

Read the two together. The ratio tells you whether to spend, payback tells you how fast you can spend it again. If payback runs past twelve months, front-load the relationship: a starter bundle, a subscription, or a second-order offer that pulls margin forward instead of waiting for it.

How to increase your customer lifetime value

Four levers move lifetime value, and they are not equal. Average order value and frequency act on the base, churn acts on the multiplier, and the product catalogue sets the ceiling for all three. Work them in that order of effort, but never forget that the fourth one caps the first three.

One warning before optimising: run the calculation on your best cohort as well as your average. On most stores the top fifth of customers carries more than half the profit, and a retention programme aimed at the average customer spends most of its budget on people who were never going to come back.

Increase average order value

The fastest lever, because it needs no new customer. Bundles, upsells at checkout and a free shipping threshold set just above your current average order value all move it within a week.

The compounding matters: a 10 EUR gain in average order value, at 4 orders a year over 3 years, adds 120 EUR of revenue per customer before anything else changes. Subscriptions push it further by turning one purchase into a scheduled one. If you are evaluating that route on Shopify, the Shopify subscription calculator shows what the plan and its fees actually cost you at your volume.

Increase purchase frequency

Frequency is where email and retention flows earn their keep: a post-purchase sequence, a replenishment reminder timed to when the product runs out, a loyalty programme that makes the second order cheaper than the first.

The arithmetic is blunt. Moving from 2 to 3 orders a year raises CLV by 50%, with no change to your margin, your price or your ad spend. That is a bigger swing than most stores get from a full creative overhaul.

Reduce churn

Churn is the variable that moves CLV the hardest, because it acts on the multiplier rather than the base. Cutting annual churn from 40% to 30% takes the average lifespan from 2.5 to 3.3 years, roughly a third more value per customer.

What moves it is unglamorous: an unboxing that looks like the product page promised, delivery estimates that hold, a support reply the same day, a win-back offer sent before the customer forgets you rather than six months after. None of it is a growth hack, all of it is measurable in the retention field above.

Start with products that attract repeat buyers

Everything above assumes your catalogue can be bought twice. Plenty cannot. Furniture, decoration and most electronics are replaced on a cycle measured in years, so no email flow will ever make them a high frequency purchase.

Products with a natural reorder cycle behave differently: skincare, supplements, coffee, pet food, anything consumed or worn out. The second purchase is built into the product instead of being extracted from the customer, and it is the single decision that raises your CLV ceiling before any optimisation.

What is customer lifetime value (CLV)?

Customer lifetime value is the total profit a business can expect from a single customer over the whole time they keep buying. It is not one order, it is every order that customer will ever place, minus the cost of serving them. Because it looks past the first sale, CLV is what tells you how much you can afford to spend to acquire a customer in the first place.

What is the CLV formula?

CLV = average order value x purchase frequency per year x customer lifespan in years x gross margin. With a 100 EUR average order, 4 orders a year, a 3 year lifespan and a 20% margin, CLV is 240 EUR. This calculator uses the same formula but derives the lifespan from your retention rate, since 1 / (1 - retention) is easier to source from your own data than a lifespan in years.

What is the difference between CLV and LTV?

There is none. CLV stands for customer lifetime value and LTV for lifetime value, and the two acronyms are used interchangeably across e-commerce and SaaS. The distinction that actually matters is revenue CLV versus margin CLV: the same customer can look worth 600 EUR on revenue and 270 EUR on margin. Always compare a margin CLV with your acquisition cost.

What is a good CLV for an e-commerce store?

It depends entirely on your category. General dropshipping stores usually land between 50 and 150 EUR, fashion between 150 and 300 EUR, beauty between 200 and 400 EUR, electronics between 300 and 600 EUR, and supplements between 400 and 800 EUR because they are consumed and reordered. A CLV is only good or bad relative to your acquisition cost, never on its own.

What is a good CLV:CAC ratio?

Three to one is the accepted minimum. A customer should bring in three times what they cost to acquire, with the remainder covering fixed costs, logistics and risk. Between 1.5:1 and 3:1 you are viable but tight. Below 1.5:1 the model does not hold, and below 1:1 every new sale makes the loss bigger. Above 5:1 you are probably under-spending on acquisition.

How do I find my average order value (AOV)?

In Shopify you will find it under Analytics, then Reports, in the sales reports. Use a full quarter rather than a single week so that one promotion does not distort the number.

How do I find my purchase frequency?

Divide the number of orders over a year by the number of unique customers who ordered in that year. Two hundred orders from a hundred customers is a frequency of 2. Shopify reports it under Analytics, Reports, in the customer behaviour section, alongside your repeat purchase rate. If you have less than a year of history, extrapolate carefully rather than optimistically.

How do I find my customer lifespan?

Customer lifespan is 1 divided by your annual churn rate, which is the same as 1 / (1 - retention rate). If 30% of your customers never come back in a year, your lifespan is 1 / 0.30, that is 3.3 years. This calculator asks for retention instead of lifespan and does the conversion for you, because retention is a number you can pull from your own order history.

How do I calculate CLV in Excel or Google Sheets?

Build five columns: customer ID, average order value, purchase frequency, lifespan in years and gross margin. In the sixth column multiply the four values, for example =B2*C2*D2*E2. Drag it down your customer list and you get a CLV per customer, which lets you sort by value and see how concentrated your profit really is.

How does profit margin affect CLV?

Directly and proportionally. Margin is a multiplier in the formula, so moving from 20% to 30% margin lifts CLV by half. It is also the reason a revenue-based CLV is misleading: a customer worth 600 EUR in revenue at a 45% margin is worth 270 EUR to you, and the difference is what decides whether a 120 EUR acquisition cost is sane.

How can I increase my customer lifetime value?

Four levers, in order of speed. Raise the average order value with bundles, upsells and a free shipping threshold. Raise purchase frequency with post-purchase email flows, loyalty and subscriptions. Cut churn with a better post-purchase experience and win-back campaigns. And pick products that are reordered by nature, because a catalogue of one-shot items caps your CLV before you start.

What is predictive CLV?

Predictive CLV uses probabilistic models, typically BG/NBD paired with a Gamma-Gamma model for order value, to estimate how many more times each customer will buy and how much they will spend. It is more accurate than a flat average and it is what large retailers run. It also needs clean transaction history and a data pipeline, so for most stores the simple formula is the right starting point.

Does CLV apply to dropshipping?

Yes, and it matters more there than anywhere else. Dropshipping margins are thin and acquisition costs keep rising, so a model that only breaks even on the first order dies quickly. Knowing your CLV tells you whether you can afford your current cost per acquisition, and pushes you towards products that get reordered instead of products that sell once.

How often should I recalculate my CLV?

Once a quarter is enough for most stores, plus any time you change pricing, product mix, shipping policy or retention strategy. If you are scaling ad spend aggressively, recalculate monthly: your acquisition cost moves faster than your retention, and the ratio between the two is what tells you when to stop.
